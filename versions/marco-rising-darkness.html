The Unlikely Death of Marco Fragale

by Michael Penta · Rising darkness

A cold winter darkness seeps down Copp’s Hill Burial Ground, punctured only by the amber glow of the streetlamps on Charter Street. Antonella is in the kitchen of the small tenement apartment boiling water for coffee. Marco emerges from the bedroom still tucking in his undershirt and buttoning his trousers.

“Buongiorno, amore.” Antonella’s eyes brighten at the sight of her husband. “The coffee is almost ready.” She places two empty mugs and a plate on the table; on the plate is a fist-sized chunk of yesterday’s bread.

Marco, shouldering his suspenders and smiling back at Antonella, takes a seat at the small table, his mind fixed on the day ahead. Ever since his Navy Yard job ended after the war, steady work in 1919 is hard to come by. But today offers hope for steady employment. Antonella’s cousin had arranged for him to assist a mason repairing a nearby foundation, a mason looking for helpers and willing to teach the trade to a hard worker.

“Don’t let the coffee get cold. And eat the bread. You have a hard day’s work ahead.” Antonella has poured the coffee and taken a seat without Marco noticing. Her words bring him back to the present. The scent of the strong coffee fills his nose. He wraps his hands around the mug to feel its warmth as he brings it to his lips. They have nothing to sweeten the bitter coffee, and it bites at his mouth.

“Core mio,” he says, knowing this small bit of bread is the last of the food in the apartment, “you eat the bread. I will be fine. I get my pay after work and will come home with groceries.”

“Marco!” She rarely uses his name. Usually he is tesoro, amore, or core mio. “Mangia!” Her lips tighten.

“But, amore, you—”

She puts an end to it before he can finish his protest. “Basta! You need your strength for work.”

Marco relents. “I promise I will take it and eat it for lunch.” The tension in Antonella’s lips gives way to a satisfied smile. He takes one last gulp of the coffee and winces. “I want to be there early to make a good impression. Maybe he will ask me to come back tomorrow. I should get going.” He hurriedly puts on his coat and stuffs the bread in his pocket.

As he spills out of the tenement building onto Charter Street, Marco feels the morning air on his exposed skin. The past week has been frigid, even for January, but this morning is noticeably warmer. A blue-black glow fills the sky. Walking toward the harbor, he hears his footsteps slap against the snowless brick sidewalks. He looks to the mouth of the Charles River, but the view is blocked by the elevated rail line.

As he turns onto Commercial Street, he peeks between the railway supports, factory buildings, and the molasses tank to steal glimpses of the ships in the harbor. He recalls watching a ship fill the tank just days ago and he remembers when they painted it brown, to cover the leaks. The tank is nearly as tall as the elevated rail line. The rumble of the train passing overhead follows him down Commercial Street to 534 where he will meet the mason.

Antonio approaches, carrying a large wooden tool chest with both hands and a kerosene lantern sitting on top. “Marco? Antonio De Luca. Take this and follow me,” he instructs Marco while placing the heavier part of his load on the ground and taking the lantern. Marco nods and lifts it with both hands. The two men enter the building, walk down the hall, and open a door to a small brick basement. Marco follows Antonio down the wood stairs. The ceiling is low and both men must duck their heads to get past the beams. Some sunlight enters through the three street-level windows but the elevated railway blocks much of it.

“Marco, put that on the far side of the room,” Antonio says as he lights the lantern to survey the cracks in the brick foundation. Marco places the tool chest on the firmly packed dirt floor and Antonio rests the lantern on top to light the workspace. Antonio says, “You can start by going out back and bringing in the lime and sand to mix the mortar. You’ll find buckets and a spigot back there too.”

Marco climbs the stairs and goes outside to find bags of lime and sand stacked knee-high. He reaches down and hoists a bag over his shoulder. The weight makes him lopsided as he walks around the building, through the front door, and down the hall. He can feel his thighs burn as he descends the stairs. He repeats this trip until he has enough lime and sand to make mortar. Then he carries down the water buckets, pushing himself to take two at a time. As the mortar comes together, it resists each turn of his shovel.

When he emerges from the building for another bag, he feels the sun on his face. It has quickly turned into a beautiful sunny day, and Marco appreciates the brief moments in the warm light outside. For a moment he is reminded of his boyhood in Avellino, sitting in the grass under the azure sky, the air filled with the scent of wild fennel. Now, he tastes the lime dust in his mouth and throat. He heaves another bag onto his shoulder. For Antonella, he can endure another trip to the basement.

By late morning, the most brutal labor is complete. Antonio beckons for Marco. “This whole section needs repointing. Let me show you how to do it.” As he walks toward Antonio, he can see the feet of people walking down Commercial Street and hear the rumble of the elevated rail line. He kneels next to Antonio, careful not to block the light. Antonio demonstrates how to remove the failing mortar and press fresh mortar into the joints. Marco watches closely. Antonio hands Marco the tools and encourages him, “You try.” Marco takes the tools, cleans out a joint, and repacks it with mortar. “Bene. Make sure to fill the joint completely so there are no voids in the next one.” Marco cleans another joint and this time packs it fully with mortar. Antonio looks over the work. “Molto bene. Now even it off with the jointer.” Marco evens it off swiftly and his confidence builds as he continues with another joint. “Bravo, Marco,” says Antonio. “I’d like to see you complete the rest of this wall. Can you do that?”

“Certo!” Marco replies. Antonio moves to the adjacent wall. They work near each other, Antonio occasionally pausing to offer praise or correction.

“It’s about time for lunch,” Antonio says. “I’ve been crouched in this basement since we started. I think I’ll take lunch outside. Will you join me?”

Marco reaches into his pocket and feels the hard bread. The pangs in his stomach are at odds with his desire to impress Antonio. “If it’s alright, I’d like to stay and finish this wall.”

Antonio agrees. “Fine by me. I will be back in under an hour. If you run into any trouble, stop and wait for me.”

Marco’s arms feel heavy as he works on the wall. Just a bit longer, then he will stop to rest and eat his bread. He promised Antonella. A rumble shakes the ground, this time followed by a groan unlike anything Marco has heard before, then a series of sharp cracks, like gunshots. He jumps to his feet and looks out the window.

“A roof?” he mutters, confused.

For a moment he cannot make sense of what he sees. A mansard roof, complete with shingles and ornate trim, but missing the rest of the building, is moving toward him. Beneath it, a black carpet stretches across the landscape. Soon the rippling carpet turns into a wave. Marco watches as people, horses, and carts disappear into the darkness.

When the wave meets the elevated railway, the full mass of the roof slams into one of the iron supports. The metal shrieks, bends, and finally buckles. A section of railway collapses into the street. Marco steps back as daylight disappears with the arrival of the wave. The windows explode with a rush of darkness and debris. Marco is knocked back and hits the ground hard.

The room is filling rapidly.

By the time he gets to his knees, half of his body is covered in this sticky mass. Where are the stairs? He turns. All he sees is the lantern. The only source of light is still burning. The tool chest beneath it is encased in black, and he knows the lantern will soon be next. He tries to run to the lantern, but the dark flood is viscous. His legs and arms burn like he is still carrying the bags of sand and lime, but he forces himself forward through the thick syrup. The light grows dim as the lantern is quickly covered and then gone.

The room is filled with darkness. Panic sets in when the flood rises past his waist. He can feel his chest expanding and contracting from his quick breaths. With each inhale comes a sweetness, something familiar. It takes another moment before it sinks in, “Madonna. Molasses.”

Marco reaches out in the darkness and finds a ceiling beam. The hewn lumber feels rough in his hand. He pulls himself along the beam to what he hopes are the stairs. He repositions his hand and pulls again. He can feel the molasses seeping into every crevice of his body.

Marco’s body aches. He feels the flood at his chest. Marco tilts his head back and presses his face up against the ceiling. The molasses seeps into his ears. “I should have eaten the bread.”

He pulls again. His arms shake. His fingers begin to slip.

“Antonella.” Her name escapes his mouth. Her face comes to him clearly: her long black hair, her big brown eyes.

“Perdonami, core mio.”

The bittersweet molasses fills his mouth and nose.
